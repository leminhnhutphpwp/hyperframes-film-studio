# ── The Ides of March
074 Caesar was told to protect himself. Suetonius says he sent away the bodyguard the Senate had given him.
075 He believed that being feared was no safer than being loved. It was a brave choice, and the last one he got to make.

076 More than sixty senators joined the plot to kill him. The leaders were Brutus and Cassius, men Caesar had pardoned and promoted.
077 Brutus was the son of Servilia, Caesar's lover. A popular story says Caesar was his father, but it is almost certainly false: Caesar was only fifteen years older.

078 That morning, says Suetonius, a soothsayer named Spurinna had warned him: beware the Ides of March.
079 On his way to the Senate, Caesar is said to have remarked that the Ides had come.
080 Spurinna answered: they have come, but they have not gone.

081 On the fifteenth of March, 44 BC, Caesar went to the meeting of the Senate.
082 The senators crowded around him as if to ask a favour. Then the daggers came out.
083 Suetonius says he was stabbed twenty-three times, and fell at the foot of a statue of Pompey.
084 The famous last words, "Et tu, Brute", were written by Shakespeare, more than fifteen centuries later.
085 Suetonius records that some said he spoke a Greek phrase, meaning "You too, child?". He adds that others said Caesar said nothing at all.
086 Plutarch says he simply pulled his toga over his head.
